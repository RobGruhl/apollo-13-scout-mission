🔒 Nobody Is Tracking You Here

The short answer for parents and scouts — and then the longer, more interesting story about how the internet actually watches people

✅ The short answer

This site has no cookies, no accounts, no ads, no tracking scripts, and no data collection. It doesn't know your name, your email, or anything about you. Your game progress is saved only on your own phone and never sent anywhere — with one tiny, fully anonymous exception when you finish the mission, explained below. Nothing here is sold, shared, or profiled — there is nothing to sell.

Don't take our word for it — this page shows you how to check for yourself. 👇

What this site remembers (and where)

When you play the mission, your browser saves three small notes on your own device using something called localStorage:

  • Your decisions — so the site remembers which choices you locked in
  • Which slides you've visited — so your progress survives closing the browser
  • Your best score — for bragging rights

That data lives in your phone the way a bookmark lives in your book. It is never uploaded — there's no server to upload it to. This site has no database and no login. If you want it gone, tap the 🔄 button at the bottom of the home page and it's erased instantly.

What about the share-your-score QR code? When you finish the mission, you can share a link with your first name, troop number, and score. Here's the neat part: that information is stored in the part of the link after the # symbol — called the fragment — and browsers never send the fragment to any server. It travels from your phone to your friend's phone inside the link itself. We couldn't collect it if we wanted to.

The exceptions, in full — three tiny pings, all anonymous: First, because this site downloads itself onto your phone (see the offline trick below), the server genuinely can't tell which pages get read. So the first time you view each page on your device, your phone sends a one-time ping to this site that says only "someone read this page" — no name, no score, nothing about you. That's how we learn which parts of the mission scouts actually read. Second, the moment you finish all 10 decisions, your phone sends one more ping — just the final score, nothing else. It's like raising your hand when someone asks the room "who got an 8?" Third, if that score earns you a rank card, one more ping says which of the four cards — /ping/card/flight-director and the like — so the Apollo Table crew knows how many of each card to keep in the box. No name, no troop, no individual choices, and all three pings go only to apollo13.quest — no other company sees them. (And since this page promises the full list: there's a hidden retro mini-game on this site — that's your only hint — and it counts the same ways, one "someone opened it" ping, one final-score ping, and the same rank-card ping, because it hands out the very same cards. If you reached it through the secret door rather than by typing the address, the "someone opened it" ping says that much too — still nothing about you. Nothing else.) If your phone has no signal, pings simply wait and try again next time you open the site. (You can watch them all in the Network tab below — full marks if you check.)

🕵️ Check it yourself (really — we insist)

Every decision slide on this site has a "Sources for Skeptics" section, because checking claims is what real engineers do. Same rule applies to this page. On a computer, open this site in Chrome or Firefox and press F12 (or right-click → "Inspect") to open the developer tools — the same tools professionals use. Then look:

  1. Application → Cookies — empty. This site sets zero cookies.
  2. Application → Local Storage — there are your decisions, sitting on your device. That's all of it.
  3. Network tab (refresh the page) — every request goes to apollo13.quest. No ad networks, no analytics companies, no social media buttons phoning home. The site doesn't talk to anyone else.
  4. The code itself — this whole site is open source on GitHub. Anyone on Earth can read every line and verify what it does. Secrets don't survive that.

🧮 One honest detail: we do count visitors

Like almost every website, this one sits behind a service (Cloudflare) that delivers the pages — and it can tell us how many people visited, roughly where from (country level), and which pages were popular. That's how we'll know how many scouts scanned the QR code at the jamboree!

Here's the difference that matters: this counting happens without cookies, without identifying anyone, and without following you. It's a turnstile at the museum door — it counts that someone came in, not who you are, and it can't see where you go afterward. Tracking, by contrast, tries to recognize you specifically and follow you from site to site. We count. We don't track.

🍪 So how do other sites track people?

Great question — and knowing the answer makes you safer everywhere. The big three techniques:

1. Cookies. A cookie is a tiny note a website asks your browser to keep and show back on every visit. Cookies have honest jobs — keeping you logged in, remembering your shopping cart. The trouble is third-party cookies: when an ad company's code is embedded on thousands of different sites, its cookie lets it recognize you on all of them. Visit a shoe store, then a news site, then a game — the same ad network saw you in all three places, and now it has a diary of your day.

2. Tracking scripts and pixels. Those "Like" buttons, comment boxes, and invisible one-pixel images on many pages? Each one is code loaded from another company's server, which means that company learns you were there — even if you never click it. Free analytics tools work the same way: the site owner gets charts, and the analytics company gets to watch the site's visitors.

3. Fingerprinting. The sneakiest one. Your browser reveals little facts about itself — screen size, fonts, time zone, graphics hardware. Any one fact is common; the combination is nearly unique, like how no two scouts have the same height, shoe size, hometown, and favorite merit badge all at once. Sites can recognize the combination — no cookie required. That's why "clearing cookies" doesn't always mean starting fresh.

Why do they do it? Mostly advertising: a profile of what you read, watch, and shop for is worth money, because ads aimed at you specifically sell more. That's the deal behind most "free" websites and apps — you pay with information about yourself. It's not necessarily evil, but it's a trade, and you deserve to know you're making it.

🛡️ How to protect yourself out there

  • Look for the padlock (HTTPS) in the address bar — it means your connection is encrypted so others on the network can't read it. (This site is HTTPS-only.)
  • Question every account. When an app demands a sign-up, ask: why does it need to know who I am? Often it doesn't — it wants to build a profile.
  • Give away less. Real name, birthday, school, location — the less you post, the less can be collected, leaked, or misused. You can't un-share.
  • Use your browser's defenses. Modern browsers can block third-party cookies and known trackers — it's usually one switch in Settings → Privacy.
  • Ask an adult before sharing personal info. Not because you're not smart — because a second opinion is how NASA catches problems too. Every decision in this mission was double-checked by somebody.
  • Be a skeptic. "This app is free!" — then how does it pay its engineers? Following the money is a superpower.
